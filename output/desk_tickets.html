Expected

First call: Inventory

Stock is the gate on this ticket, so the Boss should go to Inventory before anything else. Every other decision depends on the answer: if the tee were on the shelf this is a two-minute ticket, and if it isn't, the shop needs a restock date before it can say anything useful to the customer. Asking Accounting first would mean reasoning about money for a sale we have not confirmed we can make.

I also expect Inventory to notice something the ticket only hints at. Invoice 501 is described as a rush reprint of CC-TEE-WHITE S — the exact SKU and size that is at zero. So the fix for the stock-out is already ordered; it is sitting unpaid. That turns a stock question into a cash question, which is the hand-off to Accounting.

Delegations I expect

Boss→Inventory   then Boss→Accounting   then Boss→Customer Service
  1. Boss → Inventory. How many CC-TEE-WHITE in size S, what else is on hand in that SKU, and if it is short, which vendor reprints apparel and how long they take.
  2. Boss → Accounting. What is invoice 501, is it really owed, and can the shop pay it without missing rent. This is the question that decides whether the reprint moves.
  3. Boss → Customer Service. Only once there is a verified answer: draft a reply that leads with "we don't have your size", gives the restock date, and offers the sizes that do exist.

A fourth hop is plausible: Inventory → Accounting at depth 2, if Inventory spots the blocked reprint itself and asks directly rather than routing back through the Boss. That is fine — it is the same question one hop earlier.

Facilities not called directly — but the rent it owns is the constraint Accounting has to respect

I do not expect the Boss to call Facilities on this ticket. There is no lease question here. Rent matters only as a number Accounting must subtract before saying $840 is affordable, and Accounting can read that itself with one tool call — cheaper than a hand-off.

Expected

First call: Facilities

This ticket is a claim, not a fact. Someone has emailed to say rent is due in two days, and the amount is not even stated in the message. The Boss should send it to Facilities first because Facilities owns the lease and is the only one who can say whether the email matches the record. Going to Accounting first would be asking "can we afford it?" before anyone has established what "it" is — and a shop that pays whatever a convincing email asks for is one forged letterhead away from a bad day.

The check is cheap and specific: the ticket carries lease_id = 1, so Facilities reads lease 1 and compares. Landlord name should match the requester, the amount should come from monthly_rent, and "2 days" should hold up against the desk date.

Delegations I expect

Boss→Facilities   then Boss→Accounting

  2. Boss → Accounting. Given a verified $2,400 due 2026-09-02, is it covered, what else is queued against the same account, and what is left afterwards.

I also think Facilities → Accounting at depth 2 is likely and reasonable: Facilities finishes verifying, immediately wants to know whether the shop can cover it, and asks directly. It makes the report more complete, and the depth cap of 2 stops it going further.

Inventory Customer Service no stock angle; the landlord is a counterparty, not a retail customer

Two agents should stay out. Inventory has nothing to do with rent. Customer Service writes to customers; correspondence with a landlord is the Boss's, and the shop should not send a cheerful service reply to the person who owns its building.

First call: Inventory

The ticket is typed price_override, which makes it look like a pricing question, and that is the trap. There are 8 size-M hoodies on the shelf against 20 requested. Accounting is a maybe, not a given. Nothing is being paid here — a discount is revenue, not spend. It only becomes an Accounting question if the Boss decides to order the missing twelve, and by then the other two tickets will have taken the balance down to around $160, which is its own answer. I would rather see the Boss put that decision on hold than spend a hand-off confirming the obvious. Facilities has no role at all.

How the agents performed on each ticket, and why

101 — Bulldog tee: good, because it refused to be helpful

The win here is a negative one. The customer wanted a size the shop does not have, and at no point did any agent soften that. Inventory read check_inventory at SKU and size and reported 0 with a shortfall of 1; the Boss closed the ticket with "size S is out of stock (0 on hand; need 1)" written into the note. adjust_inventory was never called, so the shop did not quietly invent the tee it had just paid to reprint.

The sharper piece of work was Inventory noticing what the plan had assumed. The plan said paying invoice 501 unblocks the reprint. Inventory checked and came back with the opposite: paying an overdue bill does not confirm a new print run, so 2026-09-05 is "only the earliest estimated restock date if a reprint is ordered; no order or delivery is confirmed". That distinction — between a bill for a reprint and a reprint — is the difference between an honest ticket and a broken promise, and it came out of a tool call, not out of the prompt.

Weak spot: the first propose_payment call (seq 445) was made with kind="vendor_invoice", which is not one of the three the tool accepts. The tool refused it, and the Boss corrected to kind="invoice" on the next call. The guard rail worked, but it cost a round trip — the model guessed at an enum instead of reading the tool's own description.

102 — Rent: the best-verified ticket of the three

This is the one where the separation of roles paid for itself. Facilities verified the lease, Accounting verified the cash, and because they are separate agents reading separate tables, the verification was genuinely independent rather than one agent agreeing with itself.

Facilities then caught something neither the plan nor I had spotted: the ticket contains no claimed amount at all. The note says only "Email: shop rent due in 2 days." So there was nothing to compare against leases.monthly_rent — it could verify the timing (2026-09-02 is two days after the desk date) but had to say the amount was unverifiable against the claim. That is a more precise answer than "the email checks out", which is what the plan expected.

The second run is the one I would point at as evidence the design works. The ticket note still read "Awaiting Boss decision" while the approval history showed the $2,400 had already posted. The Boss reconciled the two and corrected the note instead of queueing a second rent payment. A stale note plus a payable obligation is exactly the shape of a double payment, and it avoided it by checking list_pending_approvals and the payment record first.

103 — Bulk discount: the best decision, at the worst price

The outcome is the strongest of the three. The Boss did not refuse the discount (my plan's prediction) and did not grant a blanket one either. It approved $52.00 per unit conditional on the full 20 and recorded it on the ticket, leaving pricing untouched at $58.00. That threads the exact needle the schema creates: pricing has no size column, so writing the discount would have given every customer $52 hoodies in every size. Keeping the offer on the ticket means the club gets the price, the shop keeps $30 over the $22 cost floor, and nobody else is affected. update_price was never called.

But it cost the most to get there: 19 requests and 106,104 tokens, against 48k–75k for the others. The reason is visible in the hand-off chain. The Boss delegated the customer question to Customer Service first; CS correctly refused to decide the price and handed it back up to the Boss; that second Boss, already at depth 2, tried to delegate onward and was refused by the depth cap. Roughly a third of that ticket's tokens went on routing rather than on answering.

A Boss that had read get_pricing and decided the offer before asking CS to write it up — which is what the plan said it should do — would have landed the same answer in two hops.

Where the plans held

The exclusions were the most accurate part. I predicted which agents should stay out of each ticket, and the team largely agreed: Facilities and CS stayed out of 101, Inventory and CS stayed out of 102, Accounting and Facilities stayed out of 103. Across all five runs the Boss started 6 times, Accounting 6, Inventory 4, Facilities 2 and Customer Service 1 — a shape that matches the work, not a team that calls everyone for everything.

The predicted first-mover was right on all three: Inventory for the stock-gated tickets, Facilities for the claim-verification one. And the reasoning held even where the route differed — Accounting read get_lease_due itself rather than delegating to Facilities for one number, which is the "don't delegate a single lookup" rule working as intended.

Where they did not

  • Every payment prediction was wrong, for a reason that post-dates the plan. The plans were written at step 6 and said the Boss would call record_payment. Step 7 removed that tool from the agents entirely. All three payment predictions became propose_payment + a human click. The plan was not wrong about the decision — $840 then $2,400 — only about who executes it.
  • Hand-offs do not stay where you put them. 103's actual chain went sideways and then upwards. Full connectivity means the Boss cannot plan the route; it can only plan the first move. Once CS could call the Boss, the shape of the ticket stopped being mine to predict.
  • I under-predicted resolution. I expected 101 to stay in_progress and 103 to sit at waiting_approval. Both resolved, on the prompt's own definition — the requester has an answer they can act on. Reading the notes, I think the agents were right and the plan was too cautious.

The thing the plan could not have predicted

The refused delegation on 103. I wrote the depth cap at step 5 as a theoretical guard against a five-agent mesh looping. On ticket 103 it actually fired, on exactly the pattern it was built for: Boss → CS → Boss → (CS). Without it, the ticket had a live cycle.

What one agent with tools would have made simpler

Most of it, honestly. The evidence for that is in the tool histogram across the five runs:

91 tool calls to answer three tickets, against a database holding one lease, one invoice, four SKUs and three tickets. A single agent holds everything it has read in one context: it would call get_today once, read the invoice once, and spend its tokens reasoning instead of re-fetching. A fair guess is that the same three outcomes were reachable in well under half of the 367,826 tokens — the 14 hand-offs are 14 nested agent runs, each paying for its own system prompt, its own role prompt, its own house rules and its own cold start.

Three other things get simpler:

  • No routing machinery. max_depth, the delegation budget, the repeat-question fingerprint and the refusal messages exist only because agents can call each other. One agent needs none of it — and 103's wasted third would not exist.
  • One transcript. Ticket 103 produced five nested agent runs; to follow it you read the chain and the depth badges. One agent is one linear conversation.
  • No stale hand-off context. 102's second run had to reconcile a ticket note written by a different agent in a previous run. A single agent carrying its own history has less of that to trip over.

What would have got harder

This is not a one-sided trade, and two things on this desk come directly from the split:

  • The role prompts are the safety boundary. Customer Service cannot approve a discount because it is a different agent with a different prompt and it hands the decision up — which is literally what happened on 103. Collapsed into one agent, "do not approve discounts yourself" becomes a line in a long prompt competing with every other line, and the model is both the asker and the approver.
  • 102's verification was independent. Facilities checked the lease without knowing the cash position; Accounting checked the cash without re-deciding the lease. One agent checking its own reasoning is not the same thing, and I doubt a single agent would have isolated "the ticket states no amount at all" — that came from an agent whose only job was comparing a claim to a table.

If I were rebuilding this for a real shop, I would keep the Boss and Accounting split — the money boundary is worth the tokens — and fold Inventory, Facilities and Customer Service into one operations agent with all the read tools. Two agents, one boundary that matters.

Three problems this team could already solve

1. A second vendor invoice arrives

Say Elm City Gifts bills $300 for a mug restock. This is ticket 101's shape with different numbers, and every tool is in place: list_invoices for the amount and overdue count, get_cash_balance and get_lease_due for whether it is affordable, propose_payment to queue it, a human to approve. The interesting part is that the team would now refuse it — the balance is $160.00, and propose_payment rejects any proposal larger than the balance before it ever reaches a human. Accounting already demonstrated this arithmetic when it reported the $160 remainder before the rent was approved.

2. A customer wants the Crest Mug

CC-MUG-CREST / OS is at qty 0 — the other stock-out in the database, and nobody has asked about it yet. The team handled exactly this for the tee: check_inventory finds the zero, list_vendors finds Elm City Gifts (specialty "mugs and small goods", 3 lead days — not Bulldog Print Co, who do apparel), get_today turns that into 2026-09-03, get_pricing gives the $14.00 list price, and update_ticket records it. The specialty match is the bit that matters: Inventory picked the apparel vendor for a tee rather than the courier, so it would pick the gift vendor for a mug.

3. The reprint arrives and has to be received, then sold

adjust_inventory is built, guarded against negative stock, and was never called in any of the five runs — correctly, because nothing physically moved. When a box of size-S tees does land, Inventory receives it with a positive delta and a reason, and ticket 101's customer can finally be served with a negative delta. The whole receive-and-sell loop is already there and tested; it has simply had no goods to move.

Three problems it could not solve — and what is missing

1. Actually ordering the reprint — and letting time pass

This is the gap the runs found by themselves. Inventory's own words on ticket 101: "no order or delivery is confirmed". The team can pay a bill for a reprint and can quote a lead time, but there is no tool that places an order — no purchase-order table, nothing that reaches the vendor, nothing that tracks an order from placed to delivered. Both 101 and 103 stall at exactly this line, which is why both end with an estimate rather than a commitment. Compounding it, the desk date is frozen at 2026-08-31, so a 2026-09-05 delivery can never actually arrive.

  • Tools needed: a purchase_orders table with create_purchase_order, list_purchase_orders and receive_purchase_order (which would call the existing adjust_inventory on arrival); a propose_purchase approval path mirroring propose_payment, since ordering commits money; and an advance_date or scheduling tool so the desk can move to the delivery date.
  • Agents needed: no new role strictly required — Inventory can own ordering and receiving. But a Scheduler agent would earn its place, because once orders have dates, something has to wake up and ask what is due today. Right now nothing in the system is aware that time exists.

2. Actually telling the customer

Customer Service drafts a customer_message in its report, and the Boss recorded the $52.00 offer on ticket 103 — but the Yale AI Club has not been told anything. There is no contact detail anywhere in the schema (tickets carry a requester name and nothing else), no outbound channel, and no record of what was sent or whether it was received. Ticket 103 is marked resolved on the strength of a note in a database the customer cannot see. That is the weakest claim on the whole board.

  • Tools needed: a contacts table joined to tickets; send_message (email or SMS) with a real delivery result; list_messages so a thread can be read back; and an approval gate on sending, for the same reason payments have one — an outbound message to a customer is irreversible.
  • Agents needed: Customer Service can own it, but sending should be split from drafting the way paying is split from proposing. Either a Comms agent that owns the channel and the thread history, or the same propose/approve pattern with a human releasing the message.

3. Taking money in

Look at the Cash tab: every row is an outflow. $3,400.00 → $2,560.00 → $160.00, and nothing in the system can move it the other way. record_payment only subtracts; payments only has kinds for invoice, rent and purchase; there is no orders table, no receipts, no deposits. So the shop can be billed but cannot be paid. Ticket 101's customer owes $28.00 and ticket 103's club would owe $1,040.00 at the approved price — neither is recorded anywhere, and the business looks like it is dying when it is actually trading.

  • Tools needed: an orders / receipts table linked to tickets and SKUs; record_sale (which would decrement inventory and credit cash in one transaction, the mirror of record_payment); list_receivables for what customers owe; and a revenue view so "can we afford this" accounts for money that is coming in, not just what is sitting in checking.
  • Agents needed: Accounting can extend to receivables, but the decisions are different enough — chasing an unpaid customer is not the same job as paying a vendor — that a Sales / Revenue agent owning quotes, orders and collections would be the cleaner split. It would also give the $52.00 offer on ticket 103 somewhere real to live: as a quote with an expiry, rather than a sentence in a ticket note.

The pattern across all three gaps is the same. This team is very good at reading the shop and deciding what should happen, and the human-approval layer makes those decisions safe to act on. What it cannot do is reach anything outside the database — a vendor, a customer, a calendar, a till. Every missing tool above is a door to the outside world, and every one of them, on the evidence of how the payment gate behaved here, should be built with the same propose-then-approve shape rather than handed to an agent directly.
